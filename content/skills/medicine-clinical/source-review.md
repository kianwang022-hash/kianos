## S2｜National Academies — Improving Diagnosis in Health Care

Sources:
https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/21794
https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/21794/chapter/4
https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/21794/chapter/6

Accepted:
- diagnosis is a complex, collaborative and iterative process;
- information gathering, clinical reasoning, testing and communication are linked;
- patients and families are part of the diagnostic team;
- education should include clinical reasoning, teamwork, communication, appropriate test use and health IT;
- diagnostic performance is affected by work-system and organizational factors;
- feedback and continuous learning are necessary for improvement.

Boundary:
- the report is a consensus framework, not a disease-specific diagnostic algorithm;
- its 2015 epidemiologic estimates are historical and should not be used as current incidence without re-checking;
- improving diagnosis is not reducible to eliminating individual cognitive bias.

## S3｜AHRQ — Probability and the Diagnostic Pathway

Source:
https://www.ahrq.gov/diagnostic-safety/resources/issue-briefs/probabilistic-thinking3.html

Accepted:
- test selection should consider likely diagnoses, concerning diagnoses, and test benefit / harm;
- test results are interpreted in the context of pretest probability;
- sensitivity / specificity or related test-accuracy information does not make a result definitive;
- post-test probability should update, not replace, clinical reasoning.

Learner use:
- supports pretest → test → post-test thinking;
- supports asking whether a test can change management.

Boundary:
- Bayesian reasoning does not require arithmetic for every bedside decision;
- exact probabilities are often uncertain;
- mathematical precision must not become confidence theater.

## S4｜AHRQ — Diagnostic safety, cognition and context

Sources:
https://www.ahrq.gov/diagnostic-safety/resources/issue-briefs/dxsafety-current-state3.html
https://www.ahrq.gov/diagnostic-safety/resources/issue-briefs/dxsafety-cognitive-load1.html

Accepted:
- premature closure, confirmation bias, base-rate neglect and availability are associated with diagnostic error;
- newer frameworks emphasize context and situativity rather than diagnosis only “in the head”;
- workload, cognitive load, physical / social / technological environments can alter diagnostic performance;
- hindsight and outcome bias complicate retrospective evaluation of reasoning.

Boundary:
- cognitive-bias labels are not adequate causal explanations by themselves;
- dual-process models are useful simplifications, not literal two-box descriptions of every reasoning act;
- after outcome is known, reasoning must be judged against information available at the time.

## S5｜GRADE Working Group + WHO Evidence-to-Decision

Sources:
https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/
https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240011908
https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-SCI-QNS-MST-2023.1

Accepted:
- certainty of evidence and strength of recommendation are distinct;
- evidence-to-decision requires explicit consideration of desirable / undesirable effects and contextual criteria;
- resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility and values may affect recommendations;
- systematic review evidence is essential but not sufficient for recommendations.

Learner use:
- prevents “highest evidence level = automatic treatment” collapse;
- supports transparent treatment reasoning.

Boundary:
- clinicians are not expected to run a full guideline-development GRADE process at bedside;
- patient-level decisions still require current guideline, disease-specific evidence, individual risk and preferences.

## S6｜NICE — Shared Decision Making

Sources:
https://www.nice.org.uk/about/what-we-do/our-programmes/nice-guidance/nice-guidelines/shared-decision-making
https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng197/chapter/Recommendations

Accepted:
- shared decision making is a joint process between clinician and patient;
- test / treatment choices should integrate evidence with the person's preferences, beliefs and values;
- discussions should include benefits, harms, consequences and the option of no treatment / no change when relevant;
- information should be delivered in manageable chunks and understanding checked.

Boundary:
- patient preference does not turn unsafe / unavailable options into appropriate care;
- emergency, capacity and legal contexts can alter the decision process;
- shared decision making is not “patient chooses alone”.

## S7｜AHRQ TeamSTEPPS — SBAR and Handoff

Sources:
https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/sbar.html
https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/handoff.html
https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/resources/modules/index.html

Accepted:
- structured communication can support concise transfer of clinically important information;
- handoff transfers information plus authority / responsibility;
- uncertainty, recent changes, response to treatment and contingency plans are important handoff content;
- closed-loop tools can reduce communication failure.

Boundary:
- SBAR / handoff templates are tools, not substitutes for clinical reasoning;
- no structure guarantees a safe handoff if the content is wrong or responsibility remains ambiguous.

## S8｜GMC — Good medical practice 2024

Sources:
https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/good-medical-practice
https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/good-medical-practice/domain-3-colleagues-culture-and-safety

Accepted:

- listen to patients and support informed decisions;
- continuity requires clear information transfer and responsibility transfer;
- delegation requires appropriate competence, instruction and access to supervision;

Boundary:
- GMC standards apply directly to its regulated jurisdictions and registrants; this book uses them as a current professional-practice reference, not as universal law;
- local professional regulation, institutional policy and supervision requirements remain current-first.

## Durable boundaries

### Differential diagnosis is prioritization, not list generation

A clinically useful differential reflects:
- likelihood;
- danger;
- actionability;
- explanatory fit;
- current evidence.

Twenty unranked diagnoses can be worse than four well-reasoned possibilities.

### Probability is useful even when imprecise

Clinical probability is often approximate.

The durable skill is to know whether probability is:
- low enough to stop;
- intermediate enough that a test can help;
- high enough that the next action changes.

Do not force false decimals.

### A test result is not a diagnosis

A result is shaped by:
- pretest probability;
- sensitivity / specificity;
- timing;
- spectrum;
- sample / measurement quality;
- threshold;
- downstream interpretation.

### Evidence is not the same as recommendation

Research evidence informs:
- effect estimates;
- uncertainty.

Recommendation adds:
- benefits / harms;
- values;
- resources;
- feasibility;
- context.

Individual care adds:
- patient-specific facts and goals.

### Guideline adherence is not the entire clinical task

Guidelines are valuable compressed evidence and recommendation products.

They may be:
- outdated;
- population-mismatched;
- conditional;
- limited by access / resources;
- silent on multimorbidity.

Use them as high-value decision support, not autonomous decision makers.

### Clinical uncertainty must produce a plan

When diagnosis is not settled, document:
- working possibilities;
- what would change the ranking;
- what cannot safely wait;
- follow-up / reassessment;
- contingency / escalation.

### Cognitive bias is not an adequate root cause

Reasoning interacts with:
- workload;
- information design;
- interruption;
- staffing;
- time;
- technology;
- team communication.

Avoid hindsight blame.

### Patient-centered does not mean boundary-free

Patient goals and preferences are essential.

Professional scope, safety, consent, law, available evidence and feasibility remain real constraints.

### Specialty competence is current-first

When future clinical training reaches a specialty:
- use current authoritative guidelines;
- use local protocols;
- use supervised real cases;
- preserve specialty-specific risk thresholds.

Do not freeze disease management details into this generic book.
